# Valuing urban density: evidence from the COVID-19 closures in London and England and Wales

Research brief by Affan, October 2026, for a Director of Studies or prospective supervisor in urban economics. All figures are estimates with sampling uncertainty, and p-values are wild cluster bootstrap p-values unless stated.

## Research question

The project asks how much young adults value living in dense places, and whether that value can be attributed to consumption variety. The COVID-19 closures temporarily removed much of the residential value of dense places and restored it on known dates. Restaurants, bars, theatres, cinemas and gyms closed from March 2020 and reopened in stages to May 2021, while offices emptied over the same months. In a triple difference that compares young adults with older residents of the same place and year, every shock common to all ages in a place, such as local housing costs and employment, is absorbed. A second question follows from the first, namely whether dense places recovered their young residents after reopening or settled into a lower equilibrium.

## Data

The migration outcome is net migration by single year of age between local authorities, from the ONS internal migration estimates for the years to June 2016 to 2025, for the 32 London boroughs and 316 districts of England and Wales. Density is measured by hospitality and leisure units per resident from UK Business Counts, which was the registered measure, and by population-weighted access to food venues from the Food Standards Agency hygiene register. Small-area work uses the 2001, 2011 and 2021 Census questions on address one year before, ONS small area population estimates rebased to Census 2021 for 7,264 MSOAs, and a closure calendar for seven venue types coded from 42 rules in the coronavirus regulations for England and Wales. Controls for teleworkability, garden access, students and density come from the Census, the ONS homeworking score and the Business Register and Employment Survey.

## Three main findings

1. Young adults left dense places in 2020 and returned in 2022, relative to older residents of the same places. In London the triple-difference coefficient is −5.24 per 1,000 per standard deviation of amenity density in the year to June 2020 (p 0.002), and the 2022 coefficient exceeds it by 13.32 (p < 0.001). Across England and Wales, with access to food venues as the measure, the 2020 coefficient is −5.54 and the 2022 minus 2020 contrast is +11.14 with teleworkability and garden access held fixed (both p < 0.001), and against the pre-2020 trend the deviations are −5.28 and +9.36. This measure was chosen after the registered measure, venues per resident, gave a contrast of −1.53 (p 0.191), and it correlates at about 0.9 with job and population density, so the response is a valuation of density as a whole.

2. Consumption variety cannot be separated from the wider bundle of density benefits with British data. Four designs were tried, three of them registered in advance in two pre-analysis plans, and each fell short for a reason tied to its source of variation. Puga's (2010) account of bundled agglomeration mechanisms is thereby extended from firms to households.

3. The registered recovery test is inconclusive, and its instrument is in doubt. The Davis and Weinstein coefficient for MSOAs at ages 25 to 34, with the 2019 to 2021 shock instrumented by access, is −0.51 with a 95 per cent interval from −1.11 to 0.10 (first-stage F 33.0), so both full recovery (−1) and a lasting shift (0) lie inside the interval. In secondary readings, registered as further estimates outside the decision rule, dense places regained their young residents faster than their ordinary momentum predicts (headline minus pseudo-shock coefficient −1.79, interval −2.54 to −1.04), and the coefficient outside London is −0.71 (interval −1.37 to −0.05), a reading of full recovery.

| Tier closures (national plan) | Extra closure days beyond national lockdowns | Interaction with density +0.24 (p 0.847) | Annual totals fail the cross-section first stage (p 0.581), and the days were set by case rates |
| Venue mix (venue-mix plan) | Variety-days lost within districts, from the mix of venue types that closed | −1.28 per 10 days (p 0.011) | The 2001 to 2011 placebo is +3.28 (p < 0.001), which fails the registered rule, and the treatment is close to an inverse takeaway share (correlation −0.92) |

In every design the available variation in variety moved with density itself or with a pre-existing neighbourhood dynamic. The venue-mix design came closest, with a significant headline that survived a stopping rule and a full set of further registered estimates, and it was rejected under its own placebo, with a large movement of opposite sign in the same neighbourhoods in the decade before the pandemic.

## The recovery test and its instrument problem

The recovery design needs an instrument that moves the 2019 to 2021 shock and affects later growth only through that shock. Access to food venues is a strong instrument in the first stage, with an F of 33.0 across MSOAs and 82.1 across districts. The registered pseudo-shock placebo, with a shock from 2015 to 2017 and a recovery from 2017 to 2019, gives a coefficient of +1.28 (interval 0.81 to 1.76), so young-adult growth in ordinary years is predicted by access beyond the pre-pandemic trend, and the exclusion restriction is doubtful. Recovery from the closures is therefore mixed in the headline with a dynamic already associated with access, and that dynamic is removed by the comparison with the pseudo shock only if it would have continued unchanged after 2021. Within London the first-stage F is 1.3, so the question of whether the largest city tipped, as Monte, Porcher and Rossi-Hansberg (2026) find for commuting in the largest US cities, cannot be answered with this instrument. Across districts the readings diverge, with −0.14 at ages 25 to 34 (a lasting shift) and −0.62 at ages 20 to 29 to 2024 (full recovery).

## Two next steps that need university data access

1. Follow the same individuals with Understanding Society under special licence. The special licence edition with local authority identifiers (UK Data Service, SN 6666) requires an accredited researcher at a university and an approved project. With it, young adults living in dense districts in 2019 could be followed through 2020 to 2023 to test whether those who left returned, whether they were replaced by new arrivals, and what reasons for moving they gave. With that test it could be settled whether the 2022 return in the aggregate data is a return of the same people, and an individual-level recovery measure free of the census anchoring in 2021 would be available.

2. Use weekly card spending and phone mobility at neighbourhood level across the tier period. Commercial card-spending and mobility data are licensed to university researchers through research data centres such as the ESRC-funded Consumer Data Research Centre, under a data-sharing agreement. In the tiers of October to December 2020, hospitality closed in some areas while workplaces stayed open, and weekly spending on meals and drinks by small area would provide variation in lost variety that moves places independently of density and of their long-run access. The same variation could be used as a recovery instrument independent of the long-run access dynamic found in the pseudo-shock placebo.

## Three questions for the reader

1. Is the stronger paper a measurement paper on the value young adults place on density, built on the national triple difference with its registered null and post hoc access result reported side by side, or is it too weakened by the post hoc choice of measure to stand alone?
2. Would a paper built around the failure to separate variety, framed as a household counterpart to Puga's problem with four documented designs, be more publishable than one that pursues separation with new weekly spending and mobility data?
3. Is the recovery question, whether dense residential places tip into a lower equilibrium after a temporary shock, the more promising direction, given that it needs an individual panel or a new instrument before the registered verdict can move beyond inconclusive?
